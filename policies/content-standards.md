## Purpose and Scope

This policy defines standards for LinkedIn content creation. It is not designed to prevent posting — it is designed to prevent the specific kind of posting that makes your connections pretend not to have seen it.

This policy applies to all LinkedIn users who have ever described themselves as a "connector," considered posting about their morning routine, or typed the words "I'm humbled to announce." It operates alongside the [Achievement Inflation Policy](achievement-inflation.html#li-achieve--the-vagueness-principle) and [AI Concealment Policy](ai-concealment.html#li-ai--plausible-deniability-framework). Use the [Should I Post This? process guide](../processes/should-i-post-this.html) before publishing.

## The Humble Brag Framework

A humble brag presents an achievement as though it were a minor personal inconvenience that yielded unexpected wisdom. "I almost didn't apply. I'm so glad I did." is the canonical example. It implies humility while ensuring the achievement is communicated clearly, twice.

Posts are permitted one achievement per post. Achievements must not be described as "humbling" unless the author experienced genuine humility, which is rare and not a synonym for "pleased." "I'm proud to announce" is permitted. "I'm honoured to share" is scrutinised. "I'm humbled" will be read as "I want you to know I'm aware of how impressive this is," which is fine, but say it directly.

Retroactive narrative significance is prohibited. If you did not, in the moment, think "this relates to resilience," you may not claim afterwards that it did.

## Prohibited Phrases and Clichés

The following phrases are suspended from use until further notice:

- **"In today's rapidly evolving landscape"** — this phrase has never added information to a sentence.
- **"Game-changer"** — nothing on LinkedIn has changed any games.
- **"I'll let the numbers speak for themselves"** followed immediately by you speaking for the numbers.
- **"I'm excited to share"** followed by something only you are excited about.
- **"We're just getting started"** — this is not a promise, it is a threat.
- **"What's your take?"** appended to a post you would like to go viral without appearing to want it to go viral.
- **"Delve"** — AI wrote this.

Posts consisting entirely of three-word sentences.  
Each on its own line.  
For emphasis.  
Are also suspended.

## Crowbarring Strategic Insights into Life Events

This refers to extracting professional lessons from personal events that did not contain them. Common examples: leadership insights from a child's football match; resilience frameworks derived from a delayed flight; business strategy attributed to cooking dinner.

A personal event may be shared without a lesson. You do not owe LinkedIn a takeaway. If the event only becomes postable once you have appended a professional insight, the insight is structural camouflage, not wisdom. Remove it. If the event is worth sharing, share it plainly.

Exception: if the lesson is genuinely specific and non-obvious — not "adversity teaches persistence" in a new costume — it may proceed. "Running a marathon taught me that marathons are very long" is specific, accurate, and welcome.

## Passive-Aggressive Congratulations Protocol

Congratulating someone who has been promoted above you is one of the more technically demanding communications scenarios on the platform. The following protocol applies.

**Step 1:** Wait at least 72 hours. Immediate congratulations signal that you were watching, which you were, but need not confirm.

**Step 2:** Choose phrasing carefully. "So well deserved" implies you considered whether it was deserved. "Couldn't happen to a better person" implies you considered other people. Both are permissible; both will be noticed.

**Step 3:** Remove all references to yourself from the message — your shared tenure, your proximity to their new role, your feelings about their trajectory. Rewrite so the subject of every sentence is the person being congratulated. "Having worked alongside [Name] for six years, I can attest to their brilliance" is a tribute in which the tribute-giver features prominently. This is noted.

**Step 4:** One emoji maximum. The 🙌 is under review but currently permitted.

## Emoji Governance Framework

Emojis are a legitimate communications tool when used to indicate tone, replace a word, or signal warmth in an otherwise dry message. They are not a substitute for having something to say.

The following limits apply per post:

- **Achievement posts** — maximum two emojis. A promotion announcement does not require 🎉🙌🚀💪🔥. It requires one sentence and perhaps a 🎉.
- **Certificate announcements** — maximum two emojis. A forty-five minute LinkedIn Learning completion does not warrant a parade. See [Achievement Inflation Policy](achievement-inflation.html#li-achieve--certification-and-credential-calibration) for full certificate guidance.
- **Congratulations messages** — maximum one emoji. This is covered separately under the Passive-Aggressive Congratulations Protocol above.
- **Opinion posts** — zero emojis. If your opinion requires emojis to convey tone, reconsider the opinion.
- **Posts about AI, disruption, or "the future of work"** — the rocket emoji (🚀) is suspended indefinitely in this context. It has been used too many times in relation to things that did not launch.

The 💯 emoji is under review. Its current approved use is limited to genuine agreement with a specific quantifiable claim. Using it to mean "I support this sentiment broadly" is not approved.

## Open Employment Signalling Standards

Open employment signalling — communicating availability for work without stating it directly — is governed by frequency and tone. Subtlety is permitted; frequency eliminates subtlety.

A single well-crafted post about skills, interests, or career direction reads as professional development. Two in a week reads as urgency. Three or more reads as a situation, which is the accurate description and should not be broadcast at scale.

**The Hyperscaler Engagement Gambit** is a specific high-risk technique in which a user comments on a post from AWS, Google, Microsoft, Meta, or a similar organisation, implying that their strategy would improve substantially if the commenter were hired. This technique has never resulted in a hire. The comment will not be read by the talent acquisition team. The observation that their Q3 earnings call missed a key opportunity in edge computing will not reach the hiring manager for the edge computing team. If you want to work there, apply.

Content that signals availability must demonstrate capability, not urgency. A post about what you have built is always more effective than a post about the fact that you built it for someone who no longer employs you.
